1. What is statistical thinking?

Statistical thinking means using data to understand variation, uncertainty, patterns, and differences. Instead of focusing on one observation, we ask what the whole dataset shows and how reliable that conclusion may be.

Population, sample, and variable

  • Population: The entire group we want to understand, such as all students in a university.
  • Sample: The subset actually observed or measured, such as 100 randomly selected students.
  • Variable: A characteristic recorded for each unit, such as height, marks, or commute travel time.
  • Variation: The fundamental fact that observations differ from one another across units and conditions.
Example

If we want to understand the average time students take to reach college, we survey a sample. The sample average estimates the population average, but it may differ because of sampling variation.

2. Distributions

A distribution describes the values a variable takes and how often or how likely those values are. A distribution can be described by its center, spread, shape, and unusual observations.

2.1 Center

  • Mean: Sum of values divided by the number of values. It uses every value and is sensitive to extreme observations.
  • Median: The middle value after sorting; for an even number of values, average the two middle values. Highly robust to outliers.
  • Mode: The most frequent value or category.
data = [2, 3, 3, 4, 18]
print(np.mean(data))   # Output: 6.0
print(np.median(data)) # Output: 3.0

The single extreme value 18 pulls the mean upward to 6.0, while the median stays near the central observations at 3.0.

2.2 Spread

  • Range: Maximum minus minimum.
  • Interquartile range (IQR): Third quartile Q3 minus first quartile Q1; describes the spread of the middle 50% of data.
  • Variance: Average squared distance from the mean, using a population or sample formula depending on the context.
  • Standard deviation: Square root of variance; expressed in the exact same physical units as the original data.

2.3 Shape

  • Symmetric: The left and right sides look roughly similar around the center.
  • Right-skewed: A long tail extends toward larger positive values; mean is typically greater than median.
  • Left-skewed: A long tail extends toward smaller negative values; mean is typically less than median.
  • Unimodal / Bimodal: One prominent peak / two prominent peaks.
  • Uniform-like: Values occur at roughly similar rates across an entire interval.

2.4 Plotting a histogram

A histogram groups numerical values into bins and shows the count or density in each interval. It is useful for seeing shape, clusters, gaps, skewness, and possible outliers. 

marks = [45, 50, 52, 55, 58, 60, 61, 65, 70, 72, 80, 92]

plt.hist(marks, bins=5, edgecolor="black")
plt.xlabel("Marks")
plt.ylabel("Frequency")
plt.title("Distribution of marks")
plt.show()

Bin choice matters: too few bins can hide patterns; too many can make random variation look important. Try a few sensible bin counts.

2.5 Other distribution visualizations

  • Box plot: Shows median, quartiles (Q1, Q3), spread, and potential outliers.
  • Density plot / KDE: Smooth continuous estimate of a distribution's shape.
  • ECDF: Shows the empirical fraction of observations less than or equal to each value.
  • Dot plot: Shows individual values directly and is especially useful for small datasets.

3. Outliers

An outlier is an observation unusually far from the rest of the data. It may be a recording error, a rare but valid event, or evidence of a different subgroup. Do not delete it automatically.

3.1 IQR rule

Calculate Q1 (25th percentile), Q3 (75th percentile), and IQR = Q3 − Q1. A common flagging rule is:

Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR
Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

Values falling outside the fences are flagged as potential outliers. This is a diagnostic rule, not proof that a value is wrong. 

data = np.array([10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14, 15, 60])

q1, q3 = np.percentile(data, [25, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
low = q1 - 1.5 * iqr
high = q3 + 1.5 * iqr
outliers = data[(data < low) | (data > high)]

print("Q1:", q1, "Q3:", q3, "IQR:", iqr, "Fences:", (low, high))
print("Potential outliers:", outliers)

3.2 What to do with an outlier

  • Check units, data entry errors, duplicate records, and sensor or measurement problems.
  • Compare the value with primary source records when possible.
  • Ask whether it is plausible in the real context.
  • Report how it affects the mean, median, spread, or model.
  • Keep valid extreme values unless there is a clear, documented reason to exclude them; always explain exclusions.

4. Summarizing distributions

A good summary usually describes center, spread, shape, and unusual observations. Choose statistics that match the shape of the data.

4.1 Common summary statistics

Statistic Meaning / formula Use
Mean Sum of values ÷ n Roughly symmetric data without extreme outliers
Median Middle sorted value Skewed data or data containing outliers
Range Maximum − minimum Very simple overall spread
Q1 / Q3 25th / 75th percentiles Position and quartile spread
IQR Q3 − Q1 Robust spread of the middle 50%
Variance Mean squared deviation from mean Spread in squared units
Standard deviation Square root of variance Spread in original measurement units

4.2 Example summary in Python

data = pd.Series([10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14, 15, 60])

print(data.describe())
print("Variance:", data.var()) # sample variance (ddof=1)
print("Std dev:", data.std())   # sample standard deviation

Pandas var() and std() use sample formulas by default (degrees of freedom = 1). NumPy's np.var() and np.std() default to population-style division by n. Always select the convention that matches the scientific question.

4.3 Reporting results

A statistical report should state the sample size, units, useful summary statistics, and the pattern shown by the plot. Avoid claiming more than the data supports.

Sample Report Statement

“For the 8 recorded values, the median was 12.5 and the distribution included one unusually large value, 60. Because the data are right-skewed, the median and IQR may describe the typical value and spread more robustly than the mean and standard deviation.”

5. Variance

Variance measures how far values spread around their mean. It averages squared deviations, so larger deviations contribute disproportionately and the result is in squared units.

5.1 Population and sample variance

Population variance: σ² = Σ(xi − μ)² / N
Sample variance (unbiased): s² = Σ(xi − x̄)² / (n − 1)

The sample formula divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) because the sample mean is estimated from the same data. This correction removes systematic downward bias under standard sampling assumptions. 

values = [2, 4, 6]
print(np.var(values))           # population variance = 2.666...
print(np.var(values, ddof=1))   # sample variance = 4.0

5.2 Hand calculation

For values 2, 4, 6:

  • Mean μ = (2 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 4.
  • Deviations from mean: (2 − 4) = −2, (4 − 4) = 0, (6 − 4) = 2.
  • Squared deviations: (−2)² = 4, 0² = 0, 2² = 4.
  • Sum of squared deviations: 4 + 0 + 4 = 8.
  • Population variance: 8 / 3 ≈ 2.67.
  • Sample variance: 8 / (3 − 1) = 8 / 2 = 4.0.

Interpretation: Variance is non-negative; zero variance means every observation is identical. Standard deviation is often preferred because it returns to the original physical units.

6. Probability mass functions (PMFs)

A probability mass function gives the probability of each possible value of a discrete random variable. For a variable X:

p(x) = P(X = x)

6.1 PMF rules

  • Each individual probability is between 0 and 1: 0 ≤ p(x) ≤ 1.
  • The probabilities over all possible discrete values sum to 1: Σ p(x) = 1.
  • A PMF applies to discrete outcomes such as number of heads, arrivals, or die rolls.

6.2 Example: fair die

For a fair six-sided die, P(X = x) = 1/6 for x ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. 

x = np.arange(1, 7)
pmf = np.ones(6) / 6

plt.bar(x, pmf, width=0.8, edgecolor="black")
plt.xticks(x)
plt.xlabel("Die result")
plt.ylabel("Probability")
plt.title("PMF of a fair die")
plt.show()

6.3 Plotting a PMF from observed data

outcomes = pd.Series([1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 4, 2, 3, 1])
pmf = outcomes.value_counts(normalize=True).sort_index()

pmf.plot(kind="bar")
plt.xlabel("Outcome")
plt.ylabel("Estimated probability")
plt.title("Empirical PMF")
plt.show()

The empirical PMF estimates probabilities from relative frequencies. A sample estimate may differ from the true probability, especially for small samples.

6.4 Limits of PMFs

A PMF is strictly for discrete variables. A continuous variable such as height or waiting time does not assign positive probability to each exact point; instead, probabilities are described over intervals using a probability density function (PDF): P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ f(x) dx (the area under the density curve from a to b).

6.5 Other visualizations

  • PMF bar chart: for discrete probabilities.
  • Histogram: for sample observations of continuous/discrete numeric values.
  • PDF / density curve: for continuous theoretical models.
  • CDF or empirical CDF: for cumulative probabilities.
  • Box plot: for compact summaries of center, spread, and potential outliers.

7. The class size paradox

The class size paradox is a sampling/averaging effect: a student selected at random is more likely to be in a large class than a small class. When asking about the class attended by a randomly selected student, large classes contribute more students and therefore receive more weight.

7.1 Simple example

Suppose one class has 10 students and another has 30 students (total 40 students).

  • If you choose a class uniformly at random, each class has a 50% chance, and the average class size is: (10 + 30) / 2 = 20.
  • If you choose a student uniformly at random, the chance of coming from the larger class is 30 / 40 = 75%.
  • The expected class size experienced by a randomly chosen student is:
    (10 × 10 + 30 × 30) / (10 + 30) = (100 + 900) / 40 = 25
    which is noticeably larger than the administrator's average of 20!

7.2 Why it matters

  • Different sampling methods answer fundamentally different questions.
  • An average across groups is not always the same as the average experienced by individuals.
  • Similar weighting distortions occur in hospitals (patient wait times vs physician averages), public transit, and online platforms.

8. DataFrame indexing

Pandas indexing selects rows and columns. The two most important indexers are loc (label-based) and iloc (integer-position-based). 

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "name": ["Asha", "Bala", "Charan"],
    "marks": [82, 75, 91],
    "city": ["Ongole", "Tirupati", "Nellore"]
}, index=["s1", "s2", "s3"])

print(df["marks"])             # one column (Series)
print(df.loc["s1"])            # row with label s1
print(df.loc["s1", "marks"])   # value at label s1, column marks
print(df.iloc[0])              # first row by position
print(df.iloc[0, 1])           # first row, second column
print(df.loc["s1":"s2"])       # label slice includes s2
print(df.iloc[0:2])            # positional slice excludes position 2

8.1 Boolean indexing

high_marks = df[df["marks"] >= 80]
print(high_marks)

selected = df.loc[df["marks"] >= 80, ["name", "marks"]]
print(selected)

8.2 Common indexing rules

  • df['column'] selects one column.
  • df.loc[row_label, column_label] uses labels.
  • df.iloc[row_position, column_position] uses integer positions.
  • df.loc[condition, columns] filters rows and chooses columns simultaneously.
  • Use .copy() if you need an independent DataFrame to modify without warnings.
  • Label slices with loc include the ending label; positional slices with iloc exclude the stop position.

9. Cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)

A cumulative distribution function gives the probability that a random variable is less than or equal to a value:

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

It accumulates probability from the smallest values upward.

9.1 CDF properties

  • F(x) is always between 0 and 1: 0 ≤ F(x) ≤ 1.
  • F(x) never decreases as x increases (monotonically non-decreasing).
  • For sufficiently small x, F(x) → 0; for sufficiently large x, F(x) → 1.
  • For a discrete variable, the CDF rises in distinct steps; for a continuous variable, it is smooth.

9.2 Example: die CDF

x = np.arange(1, 7)
cdf = np.cumsum(np.ones(6) / 6)

plt.step(x, cdf, where="post")
plt.scatter(x, cdf)
plt.ylim(0, 1.05)
plt.xlabel("Die result x")
plt.ylabel("P(X <= x)")
plt.title("CDF of a fair die")
plt.show()

# For a fair die, F(3) = P(X <= 3) = 3/6 = 0.5

9.3 Empirical CDF

For observed values x1, …, xn, the empirical CDF at x is the fraction of observations less than or equal to x. 

data = np.array([2, 3, 3, 5, 8])
sorted_data = np.sort(data)
y = np.arange(1, len(data) + 1) / len(data)

plt.step(sorted_data, y, where="post")
plt.xlabel("Value")
plt.ylabel("Fraction <= x")
plt.title("Empirical CDF")
plt.ylim(0, 1.05)
plt.show()

9.4 CDF versus PMF

PMF CDF
Probability at each exact discrete value: P(X = x) Cumulative probability up to x: P(X ≤ x)
Values sum to 1 across all discrete outcomes: Σ p(x) = 1 Values rise from 0 toward 1 and never decrease
Often shown with vertical bars or stems Often shown as a step function (discrete) or smooth curve (continuous)

10. Percentile-based statistics

The p-th percentile is a value at or below which approximately p% of observations fall. The 50th percentile is the median; the 25th and 75th percentiles are Q1 and Q3. 

data = [12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 30, 35]
print(np.percentile(data, 25))  # Q1
print(np.percentile(data, 50))  # Median
print(np.percentile(data, 75))  # Q3

10.1 Percentile rank

A percentile rank describes the percentage of observations at or below a given score. If a score is at the 90th percentile rank, it is higher than or equal to roughly 90% of the reference observations.

10.2 Comparing percentile ranks

Raw scores may not be comparable when groups have different distributions. A percentile rank gives a relative position within a chosen reference group, but it does not say how large the absolute numerical difference is.

Example

A score of 80 could be at the 90th percentile in an easy exam, but at the 70th percentile in a different, higher-scoring cohort. Percentiles depend strictly on the comparison population.

10.3 Percentiles in Python

scores = np.array([55, 60, 62, 70, 75, 80, 90])

p90 = np.percentile(scores, 90)
print("90th percentile:", p90)

# Approximate fraction at or below a chosen score
score = 75
percentile_rank = 100 * np.mean(scores <= score)
print("Percentile rank:", percentile_rank)

11. Random numbers and simulation

Random numbers help model uncertainty, test algorithms, and simulate outcomes. A pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) produces a sequence that behaves like random data but can be reproduced deterministically using a fixed seed. 

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

uniform_values = rng.random(5)            # values in [0, 1)
die_rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=10)   # integers from 1 to 6
normal_values = rng.normal(loc=0, scale=1, size=1000)

print(uniform_values)
print(die_rolls)

11.1 Common random generators

  • rng.random(n): n values uniformly sampled from [0, 1).
  • rng.integers(low, high, size=n): random integers in [low, high) (high excluded).
  • rng.normal(loc, scale, size): normal samples with mean loc and std dev scale.
  • rng.exponential(scale, size): exponential samples where scale is the mean (1/rate).
  • rng.lognormal(mean, sigma, size): samples whose natural logarithms are normally distributed.

11.2 Reproducibility

Set a seed when you want the exact same random sequence to appear again, for example while teaching, debugging, or verifying an experiment. A seed does not make the data truly random; it makes the pseudo-random sequence repeatable.

12. Modeling distributions

A distribution model is a mathematical description of how values occur. A model can help estimate probabilities, simulate data, and compare observed patterns with an expected theoretical pattern.

12.1 Normal distribution

The normal distribution is a symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution defined by mean μ and standard deviation σ. The mean, median, and mode are equal for an ideal normal distribution.

The 68–95–99.7 Empirical Rule:

  • About 68% of values lie within 1σ of the mean (μ ± 1σ)
  • About 95% of values lie within 2σ of the mean (μ ± 2σ)
  • About 99.7% of values lie within 3σ of the mean (μ ± 3σ)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.normal(loc=50, scale=10, size=2000)

plt.hist(x, bins=30, density=True, alpha=0.6, edgecolor="white")
plt.title("Simulated normal distribution")
plt.xlabel("Value")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.show()

12.2 Z-score

A z-score expresses how many standard deviations a value is from the mean:

z = (x − μ) / σ

x, mu, sigma = 70, 60, 5
z = (x - mu) / sigma
print(z) # 2.0 standard deviations above the mean

12.3 Exponential distribution

The exponential distribution models non-negative waiting times between events in a constant-rate Poisson process. It is right-skewed and has its highest density near zero.

With rate λ > 0, the density is f(x) = λe−λx for x ≥ 0, and the mean is 1 / λ. In NumPy, the scale parameter is 1 / λ, which equals the mean. 

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
waiting = rng.exponential(scale=5, size=2000) # mean 5

plt.hist(waiting, bins=30, density=True, alpha=0.6)
plt.title("Simulated exponential waiting times")
plt.xlabel("Waiting time")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.show()

12.4 Lognormal distribution

A lognormal distribution is a positive-valued distribution where the natural logarithm of the variable follows a normal distribution. It is often strongly right-skewed and models quantities formed by multiplicative growth, such as income, asset size, or duration data.

If ln(X) is normal with parameters μ and σ, then X is lognormal. In NumPy, rng.lognormal(mean=μ, sigma=σ) uses μ and σ for the underlying normal logarithm, not the arithmetic mean of X. 

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
sizes = rng.lognormal(mean=2, sigma=0.5, size=2000)

plt.hist(sizes, bins=30, density=True, alpha=0.6)
plt.title("Simulated lognormal values")
plt.xlabel("Value")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.show()

12.5 Compare the three distributions

Distribution Shape / support Example use
Normal Symmetric; any real value (−∞ to +∞) Measurement variation, errors, physical dimensions
Exponential Right-skewed; x ≥ 0 Waiting time between events at a constant rate
Lognormal Right-skewed; x > 0 Positive quantities with multiplicative variation (incomes, city sizes)

12.6 How to assess a model

  • Plot the observed data using a histogram, density plot, or empirical CDF.
  • Compare the observed shape with the model's expected shape.
  • Check whether the model allows impossible values for the problem (e.g. negative waiting times).
  • Use domain knowledge; a visually similar curve does not prove the model is correct.
  • Where appropriate, use diagnostic plots (Q-Q plots) or formal goodness-of-fit tests.

13. Common mistakes

  • Using mean alone: Misleading when data are highly skewed or contain extreme values. Use median and IQR alongside mean and std.
  • Calling every IQR-flagged point an error: Flagged points are candidates for investigation, not automatic deletions.
  • Confusing variance with standard deviation: Variance is in squared units; standard deviation is in original units.
  • Mixing sample and population variance formulas: Use N for full populations, n − 1 for sample estimates.
  • Treating a histogram as a PMF: Histogram bins cover continuous intervals; a PMF assigns probabilities to discrete values.
  • Assuming a continuous variable has positive probability at one exact point: For continuous variables, P(X = x) = 0; probability exists only across intervals.
  • Thinking a CDF can decrease: A CDF is monotonically non-decreasing.
  • Confusing percentile value with percentile rank: A percentile is a cutoff value on the measurement scale; percentile rank is a relative percentage position (0–100%).
  • Assuming a random seed alters randomness: A seed simply makes a pseudo-random sequence deterministic and reproducible.
  • Choosing a distribution based on a tiny histogram: Small samples exhibit random fluctuations; evaluate larger datasets or domain theory.

15. Exam practice questions

Q1. Define a distribution and list four ways to describe it.
Answer: A distribution describes the values a variable takes and their frequencies or probabilities. Four ways to describe it are: 1) Center, 2) Spread, 3) Shape, and 4) Unusual values / outliers.
Q2. Explain how a histogram represents a distribution.
Answer: A histogram divides the numeric variable range into equal bins and plots vertical bars whose heights represent frequency counts or density in each interval.
Q3. What is an outlier? Explain the IQR rule.
Answer: An outlier is an observation unusually distant from other data points. The IQR rule flags any point less than Q1 - 1.5 × IQR or greater than Q3 + 1.5 × IQR.
Q4. Differentiate mean and median when a dataset is right-skewed.
Answer: In right-skewed data, extreme values in the right tail pull the mean upward, making Mean > Median. The median remains robust and resistant to extreme observations.
Q5. Define variance. Differentiate population variance and sample variance.
Answer: Variance is the average squared deviation of values from their mean. Population variance divides by N, while sample variance divides by n − 1 to provide an unbiased estimate of the population variance.
Q6. What is a PMF? State its properties and give a die example.
Answer: A PMF gives the probability of each discrete outcome: p(x) = P(X = x). Properties: 0 ≤ p(x) ≤ 1 and Σ p(x) = 1. For a fair six-sided die, p(x) = 1/6 for x ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
Q7. Explain the class size paradox with a numerical example.
Answer: If Class A has 10 students and Class B has 30 students, the dean's average class size is (10+30)/2 = 20. But sampling a student at random gives expected class size: (10×10 + 30×30)/40 = 25, because 75% of students sit in the larger class.
Q8. Differentiate Pandas loc and iloc.
Answer: loc selects rows and columns using index and column labels (inclusive endpoint). iloc selects using integer positions (0-indexed, exclusive endpoint).
Q9. Define the CDF and list its important properties.
Answer: F(x) = P(X ≤ x). Properties: 1) Monotonically non-decreasing; 2) Bound between 0 and 1; 3) F(x) → 0 as x → -∞ and F(x) → 1 as x → +∞.
Q10. Differentiate PMF and CDF.
Answer: PMF gives the probability of an exact individual discrete outcome P(X = x). CDF gives the cumulative probability up to that threshold P(X ≤ x).
Q11. What is a percentile? What is a percentile rank?
Answer: A percentile is a data cutoff value below which a given percentage of data falls. Percentile rank is the percentage of values in a distribution that are less than or equal to a given value.
Q12. Why is a fixed random seed useful?
Answer: It makes pseudo-random experiments, model training, and simulations 100% reproducible across different runs and systems.
Q13. Explain the normal distribution and the 68–95–99.7 rule.
Answer: A symmetric bell curve defined by μ and σ. Approximately 68% of data falls within μ±1σ, 95% within μ±2σ, and 99.7% within μ±3σ.
Q14. Describe the exponential distribution and its mean.
Answer: Models waiting times between events in a memoryless Poisson process with rate λ. Its support is x ≥ 0, right-skewed, and its mean is 1 / λ.
Q15. What is a lognormal distribution? How is it related to the normal distribution?
Answer: A distribution of positive values whose natural logarithm ln(X) follows a normal distribution. Arises from multiplicative random growth.
Q16. Name two plots that help compare observed data with a proposed distribution model.
Answer: 1) Histogram overlaid with model density curve (or KDE); 2) Empirical CDF compared against theoretical CDF (or Q-Q plot).
